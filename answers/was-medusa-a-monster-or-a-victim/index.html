Was Medusa a Monster or a Victim?

The popular version: Medusa was one of three Gorgon sisters, a monster with snakes for hair and a gaze that turned people to stone. Perseus killed her and used her head as a weapon. That is the retelling. The primary sources contain something the retelling removed.

Pindar, in Pythian Ode 12 (498 BCE), refers to Medusa as beautiful before her transformation. Hesiod's Theogony, from roughly 700 BCE, describes Medusa as the mortal Gorgon — her sisters Stheno and Euryale were immortal, but Medusa could die. Ovid's Metamorphoses, writing in the 1st century BCE, provides the account that the popular version is based on: Medusa was a priestess of Athena. Poseidon assaulted her in Athena's own temple. Athena's response was to punish Medusa — not Poseidon.

Ovid is a late source — writing 700 years after Homer. But the pattern he describes, of a victim punished for being assaulted in a sacred space, fits the logic of ancient Greek religious pollution law. The temple was made impure by the act. The priestess, as the human present, bore the pollution. This is not how modern readers understand justice, but it is how Greek religious law understood it.

The result is a character who functions as a monster in the Perseus story but reads, in the sources, as a tragedy wearing a hero's mask. Perseus does not defeat an evil creature. He decapitates a woman who was punished for surviving something done to her. The Gorgon face — the weapon her head becomes — is the face of someone turned monstrous by the system that was supposed to protect her.

Whether this reading is in Hesiod, Pindar, or Ovid, or whether it emerges from reading all three together, it is in the primary sources. It is not a modern invention.
