Why Is Delos Sacred in Greek Mythology?

Delos was sacred because Apollo was born there—and this birthplace made it the religious center of the entire Greek world, so important that no one was allowed to be born or die on the island itself.

The Birth of Apollo

The Homeric Hymn to Apollo (7th century BCE) gives us the oldest surviving account. The hymn describes how Leto, pregnant with Apollo, wandered the earth looking for a place to give birth. Most islands refused her—they were afraid of what a god's birth might bring. Delos, a small rocky island, accepted her. As reward, Apollo made Delos the religious center of Greece. The hymn says it directly: no island would be more honored or prosperous.

This wasn't mythology invented in hindsight. Linear B tablets from Mycenaean times (~1400 BCE) already name Delos as sacred. The Homeric Hymn codified what was already an ancient tradition.

The Island Where Nothing Could Begin or End

Here's the strange part: Greeks treated Delos so sacredly that pregnant women couldn't give birth there, and the dying had to be removed before death. You couldn't begin life or end it on Apollo's island. This wasn't superstition—it was how the Greeks showed respect. The god's birthplace had to stay pure, untouched by the messiness of human mortality.

What Most People Get Wrong

We usually credit Herodotus or later Classical writers with "discovering" Delos's importance. Wrong. Those writers were recording something already ancient. The Homeric Hymn to Apollo (7th century) already treated it as a universal religious fact. Go back further and you find Mycenaean references. Delos wasn't made important by literature—literature preserved something that had been important for centuries.
