Why Did Heracles Complete the Twelve Labours?

According to the most widely cited version of the myth, Heracles killed his wife Megara and their children during a fit of madness sent by Hera. Hera's hatred of Heracles originated at his birth: Zeus had fathered him through the mortal woman Alcmene, and Hera's jealousy toward Zeus's illegitimate offspring was a persistent theme throughout the Heracles cycle. After the murders, Heracles traveled to Delphi to consult the Oracle and ask how he could purify himself. The Oracle instructed him to serve King Eurystheus of Tiryns for twelve years and complete whatever labours Eurystheus commanded. Completion would grant both purification and, the Oracle promised, immortality. The servitude to Eurystheus was itself a consequence of Hera's interference: at the moment of Heracles' birth, Hera delayed his delivery and caused his cousin Eurystheus to be born first, making Eurystheus king by right of primogeniture rather than Heracles. The oldest source treating the madness and its consequences is Pindar's Isthmian Odes (early 5th century BCE). Euripides' tragedy Heracles (circa 416 BCE) inverts the traditional order, placing the madness after the labours rather than before.
