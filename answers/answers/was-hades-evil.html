Was Hades Evil?

No. Hades wasn't evil in ancient Greek mythology. He was strict, isolated, and ruled the dead with impartial justice. The evil version you know? That's a Christian invention pasted onto Greek sources centuries later.

What the Oldest Sources Actually Say

Homer, writing in the 8th century BCE, gives us the oldest description of how the Greeks actually saw Hades. In the Iliad (9.158-159), during an assembly of the gods, Homer calls Hades "the most implacable and hateful of the gods to mortals"—but then immediately clarifies: not because he's cruel, but because he never changes his mind, never shows mercy, and never accepts gifts. He's hated because he's inflexible, not because he torments people.

That's the key: Hades is unpopular, not evil. There's a difference.

The Homeric Hymn to Demeter (7th century BCE) shows Hades as a reasonable negotiator. When Persephone's mother demands her daughter back, Hades doesn't refuse—he works out a compromise. He's not vindictive. He's lawful.

What Most People Get Wrong

The "evil Hades" comes from Christian theology. Medieval Christian writers needed Hades to be Satan, the underworld to be Hell. They took the Greek underworld—which was neutral, not punitive—and twisted it into a torture chamber. Then they cited ancient sources as proof.

But the Greeks didn't see it that way. The underworld was where you went. Some parts were pleasant (the Elysian Fields), some were boring (the Asphodel Meadows), and yes, some were punitive (Tartarus). But Hades himself? He was just the administrator. Think of him as a cosmic accountant, not a demon.

Primary Sources

  • Homer, Iliad 9.158-159 (c. 750 BCE) — The oldest written description of Hades' character
  • Homeric Hymn to Demeter (c. 650-550 BCE) — Shows Hades negotiating fairly with Demeter
  • Hesiod, Theogony (c. 700 BCE) — Describes Hades as one of the three equal rulers of the cosmos
