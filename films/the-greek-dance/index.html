The dance from a movie

The dance that isn't real, and the one that's too real, you know this dance, even if you've never been to Greece. Arms across the shoulders, slow steps, the music builds, the steps get faster, and suddenly everybody thinks they're Greek. There's just one problem, that dance wasn't waiting in some ancient Greek village to be discovered. It was created for a movie, and one of the reasons it looks the way it does. Anthony Quinn hurt his foot, but here's where the story gets interesting, because calling it fake would be wrong too.

In 1964, Zorba the Greek gave audiences around the world an image of Greece that would become almost impossible to separate from the country itself. Anthony Quinn, Alan Bates, a beach in Crete, Mikis Theodorakis's music, and that dance. Sirtaki. Except Sirtaki wasn't some ancient dance that had survived untouched for centuries. The dance was created for the film, drawing from existing Greek dance traditions. Giorgos Provias handled the choreography, and then Anthony Quinn injured his foot.

But Quinn later recalled that the injury left him unable to perform the jumping and hopping movements expected of him. But he discovered something he could do as well, he could drag the injured foot. The scene stayed, the music became famous, the dance became famous, and millions of people around the world learned to recognize Greece through something that had just been created for a movie.

Greece has many dances

Which sounds almost ridiculous, because Greece already had more dances than Hollywood could ever fit into one film. We talk about Greek dancing, as if somebody designed one set of steps for the entire country, they didn't. Greece has regions, islands, mountains, villages, refugee communities, local histories, and those histories move differently. On the mainland, traditions like Tsamiko developed their own regional identities. Crete has dances like Pentozali. Across the Dodecanese and other islands, you find different forms of sousta.

Pontic Greek communities preserve completely different movement traditions. And even dances carrying the same name can change from one community to another. That's important. Because the map matters. A dance wasn't just something people performed. It could tell you where people came from, who they celebrated with, what community they belonged to. Sometimes even which village taught them the steps. Greek dance isn't decoration sitting on top of a Greek culture. It's one of the places culture was stored.

Nobody packs a dance into a suitcase

But maps have a problem. People leave them. War. Poverty. Displacement. Work. Opportunity. Sometimes survival. For generations, Greeks left villages, islands, and cities and built lives somewhere else. Sometimes somewhere else in Greece. Sometimes much further. Europe, Australia, Canada, the United States. And nobody packs a dance into a suitcase. There isn't a box marked “dance.” Family, photographs, clothes, important documents, 300 years of village dancing. People carried it another way. They remember it. They teach it.

One person watches another. A child watches a parent. A grandchild watches a grandparent. Someone gets pulled onto a dance floor at a wedding. Someone stands at the edge of a church festival until an uncle grabs their arm and says, come on. And suddenly something that once belonged to a particular place can survive thousands of miles away from it. Because now it isn't simply dancing. It's evidence of where you came from.

Zeibekiko and rebetiko

But then there's a dance that doesn't work anything like the image Hollywood would give it and show to the world. One dancer. No line of people moving together. No fixed sequence that everybody has to copy. No smiling roll of tourists with their arms across each other's shoulders. Zeibekiko, usually described as a solo improvisational dance. Zeibekiko leaves far more to the individual dancer. But history is much harder to fit into a neat sentence.

And this is where the story gets interesting again. Because Zeibekiko didn't begin with the 1922 Asia Minor catastrophe. Its history reaches further east into the Zeybek traditions of western Anatolia. Then history moved people. After 1922, Greek refugees from Asia Minor arrived in Greece carrying whatever pieces of home they could preserve. Language, food, music, memory, and dance. Zeibekiko traveled and changed with them. And in the growing urban world of Greece it became deeply connected with another tradition, Rebetiko.

Rebetiko developed in the urban world of the early 20th century Greece. Ports, working class neighborhoods, refugee communities, people living at the edges of respectable society. And the songs weren't exactly tourism advertisements. Love, hardship, prison, drugs, poverty, longing, everyday life. Rebetiko absorbed influence from different communities and musical traditions and became part of the changing sound of urban Greece. Zeibekiko became deeply associated with that world.

When the music was censored

And eventually authorities noticed. Because governments have a habit of becoming interested in culture when culture refuses to behave itself. In the 1930s, the dictatorship of Ioannis Metaxas imposed censorship on Greek music. Lyrics could be targeted. Recordings could be censored. Elements authorities associated with an undesirable eastern or underworld identity came under pressure. When Rebetiko changed, musicians adapted. The music survived. Then Greece went through occupation, war, civil conflict, reconstruction. Greek popular music changed again.

Zorba, and a ban at home

Which brings us back to the beginning. Mikis Theodorakis, Zorba the Greek, 1964. The music travels around the world. The film travels around the world. Sirtaki travels around the world. Millions of people hear that music and see that dance and associate it with Greece. And then, three years later, 1967. The military dictatorship takes power in Greece. And the music of Mikis Theodorakis is banned. Think about that for a second. One of the men responsible for creating one of the world's most recognizable musical images of Greece had his music prohibited inside Greece itself.

Outside the country: Zorba, the beach, the music, the dance, freedom. Inside Greece, something much more complicated. And while governments could censor music, they couldn't completely control what happened next. Because Greek culture had already traveled far beyond Greece.

Greece in Chicago

Come to Chicago, thousands of miles from Epirus, from Crete, from the islands, from Pontus. A child walks into a Greek church hall or cultural center. Maybe that child was born in Illinois. Maybe their parents were too. Maybe their Greek isn't perfect. Maybe they've never lived in the village their family came from. Maybe they've never even seen it. But somebody starts the music and somebody teaches them the steps. Organizations like the Orpheus Hellenic Folklore Society here in the Chicago area teach Greek dances and music to adults and younger generations preserving repertoire from regions across Greece.

Think about what that actually means. Something once tied closely to geography survives after the geography is gone. For a few minutes, Epirus can exist in Illinois. Crete can exist here. The islands can exist here. Pontus can exist here. Not because Illinois suddenly became Greece. Because somebody remembered and somebody taught the next person.

Who taught you yours?

Growing up, I thought Greek dancing was just something Greeks did. Weddings, festivals, church events. Music starts. Everybody gets up. Never thought very hard about why. I definitely never thought of it as an archive. But that's what I started to see. An archive that doesn't sit on a shelf, it lives in people. Every time someone remembers the steps, something survives. Every time somebody teaches a child, something moves forward. Every time nobody teaches the next person, something gets a little closer to disappearing.

Which brings us back to the beach in 1964. Sirtaki wasn't ancient. It wasn't carried unchanged through villages for a thousand years. It was created for a film from existing Greek dance traditions. Then people saw it, people danced it, Greeks danced it, tourists danced it. Festivals danced it. The world recognized it. And eventually it became part of Greece's international cultural identity. So calling it fake doesn't really solve anything. Because culture doesn't work like that.

Something invented can become tradition. Something old can change. Something imported can be adapted. Something forgotten can be revived. And something genuinely ancient can disappear completely if nobody teaches it. Age doesn't guarantee survival. People do. So maybe the question isn't “what's the real Greek dance?” There are too many answers. The better question is: who taught you yours? Because somewhere behind those steps is a place. A family, a migration, a piece of history.

And somebody who decided it was worth remembering. Maybe it was your father, your mother, your grandparents, an uncle, a teacher. Someone at a church festival who grabbed your hand and pulled you into the line. And maybe you never asked them where they learned it. Ask them while you still can. Because one day somebody younger may ask you the same question. And then it'll be your turn to remember. That's the part I was never taught.

And I'm starting to think that this part's worth preserving. This is Krios, the living story of Greek civilization. Preserve, teach, understand, connect. Pass this forward.

What the sources tell us

Established

  • Sirtaki was choreographed for Zorba the Greek (1964) by Giorgos Provias, drawing on existing Greek dances rather than surviving from antiquity.

    Wikipedia: Sirtaki

  • Zeibekiko is a solo, improvised dance whose roots reach into the Zeybek traditions of western Anatolia, before the 1922 Asia Minor Catastrophe. Refugees carried it into Greece.

    Wikipedia: Zeybek (dance)

  • The Metaxas dictatorship of the 1930s censored Greek music, including rebetiko lyrics and recordings.

    The Metaxas Project

  • The music of Mikis Theodorakis was banned inside Greece by the 1967 to 1974 military dictatorship.

    Greek Reporter

  • The Orpheus Hellenic Folklore Society in the Chicago area teaches dances from across Greece to adults and younger generations.

    Orpheus Hellenic Folklore Society

Interpretation

  • The story of Anthony Quinn's injured foot shaping the dance comes from Quinn's own later account. It is widely repeated, but it is his recollection.

    Anthony Quinn, interview at the National Film Theatre, London, 1995 (The Guardian)
