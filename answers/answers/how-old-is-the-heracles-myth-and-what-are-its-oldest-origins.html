How Old is the Heracles Myth and What Are Its Oldest Origins?

Primary Sources: The Timeline

Heracles appears in Homer's Iliad as already ancient—a figure from a previous, more violent age that current heroes measure themselves against. But the canonical twelve labours you know today? That's a late development. Pindar in the fifth century BCE lists only some of them. The fixed list of twelve doesn't appear complete until Apollodorus, writing around the first or second century CE. That's roughly seven hundred years after the myth was circulating. For most of Greek history, there was no definitive sequence. Storytellers added and removed tasks depending on what they needed the myth to do.

The Mycenaean Linear B tablets—clay records from roughly 1400 BCE—reference figures with Heracles-type attributes, though scholars still debate the direct connection. What's certain is that by Homer's time, Heracles was already old.

The Deeper Origins

Heracles is older than Greece itself. The deeper root extends back to Mesopotamian tradition. The hero Gilgamesh shares the same basic architecture: superhuman strength, divine parentage, a companion whose death breaks him, a journey to the edges of the world, an encounter with death itself. Scholars including Martin West have traced direct lines of influence from Near Eastern epic tradition into Greek mythology. Heracles isn't purely a Greek invention—he's the endpoint of a very long tradition of stories about men who are more than human and less than gods.

What the Sources Miss

There's irony built into the name itself. Heracles means "glory of Hera"—the goddess who spent his entire life trying to destroy him. According to Apollodorus, the Delphic Oracle gave him this name specifically to appease her. That naming was an attempt at reconciliation that clearly didn't work. The contradiction was encoded into his identity from birth.

The myth is at least three thousand years old in its roots, though the version you know crystallized much later. Heracles represents something deeper than Greek storytelling alone—he's part of a much older conversation about heroes, suffering, and transcendence that echoes across cultures.
