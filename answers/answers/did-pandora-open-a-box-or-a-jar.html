Did Pandora open a box or a jar?

Pandora opened a jar—specifically a large storage vessel called a pithos—not a box. This matters because the jar was meant to hold and contain something dangerous, while a box is just what we imagine when we hear the story secondhand.

What Hesiod Actually Wrote

Our earliest source is Hesiod's Works and Days, written around 700 BCE. He describes it this way: Pandora was given a large jar by Zeus, and when she opened it, all the evils of the world escaped. Hesiod uses the word pithos—a massive earthenware vessel Greeks used for storing grain, wine, or oil. Think of something you couldn't carry easily, not something you'd slip in a pocket.

The exact passage (lines 94-95) describes Zeus giving Pandora this vessel as a gift, sealed and hidden. When she opens it out of curiosity, the troubles pour out.

Where "Box" Comes From

The box version? That's Renaissance and later European translators and artists doing what they do—simplifying for their audience. A box fit better into paintings and smaller narratives. By the time the story reached popular English culture, "Pandora's box" had become the default phrase. It's catchier. It fits on a greeting card. But it's not what Hesiod said.

What Most People Get Wrong

People think of a small decorative box—maybe something precious or ornate. That completely changes how we picture the story. A pithos is industrial, functional, serious. It's not a trinket or a mystery box. It's a storage container for something meant to stay contained. The fact that it's large and heavy makes Pandora's act of opening it feel more deliberate, less accidental.

Also, many versions say Pandora "let out" evil. Hesiod makes it clear the jar itself was the prison. The container mattered.

Primary Sources

  • Hesiod, Works and Days (c. 700 BCE) — Lines 60-105. This is your ground zero. No speculation, no later retellings.
  • Ovid, Metamorphoses (8 CE) — Mentions the jar/box story but adds Roman flavor. Later source, useful for comparison.

Why This Matters

If you want to know what Greeks actually believed about this story, you start with what Hesiod said: a jar, not a box. Everything else is translation, interpretation, or reimagining. And that's fine—myths evolve. But the original hit different.
