What Was the Minotaur Myth Based On?

The dominant modern interpretation connects the Minotaur myth to the Minoan civilization of Bronze Age Crete (3000-1100 BCE). The palace complex at Knossos, first excavated by Arthur Evans beginning in 1900, is a massive multi-story structure covering over 20,000 square meters with hundreds of rooms connected by winding corridors. Evans named the civilization Minoan after the mythological King Minos. The Minoans practiced bull-leaping, acrobatic performances in which athletes vaulted over charging bulls, documented in frescoes, seals, and bronze figurines throughout the archaeological record. Bull iconography is pervasive in Minoan art. Some scholars propose the myth of a monster requiring human tribute may preserve a distorted memory of Minoan political dominance over mainland Greek communities, with tribute being a misremembering of trade relations or political subjugation. The word labyrinth may derive from labrys, the pre-Greek word for double-axe, a sacred Minoan symbol found throughout Knossos. The oldest literary source for the Minotaur is fragmentary; the most complete version appears in Apollodorus' Library (1st-2nd century CE), though the myth was clearly ancient and widely known well before that text.
