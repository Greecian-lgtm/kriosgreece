The Greek Dance That Wasn't Real… Until It Was

October 11, 2026 · 11 min

Sirtaki was created for a film. So what makes a tradition real, and who keeps it alive?

You know this dance, even if you've never been to Greece.

Arms across the shoulders. Slow steps. The music builds, the steps get faster, and suddenly everybody thinks they're Greek.

There's just one problem: that dance wasn't waiting in some ancient Greek village to be discovered. It was created for a movie.

The story

In 1964, Zorba the Greek gave audiences around the world an image of Greece that would become almost impossible to separate from the country itself.

Greece already had more dances than Hollywood could ever fit into one film. Greek dance isn't decoration sitting on top of a Greek culture. It's one of the places culture was stored.

And nobody packs a dance into a suitcase. People carried it another way. They remember it. They teach it. A child watches a parent. A grandchild watches a grandparent.

Something invented can become tradition. Something old can change. And something genuinely ancient can disappear completely if nobody teaches it. Age doesn't guarantee survival. People do.

Quoted from the film's recorded narration.

What the sources tell us

Established

  • Sirtaki was choreographed for Zorba the Greek (1964) by Giorgos Provias, drawing on existing Greek dances rather than surviving from antiquity.

    Wikipedia: Sirtaki

  • Zeibekiko is a solo, improvised dance whose roots reach into the Zeybek traditions of western Anatolia, before the 1922 Asia Minor Catastrophe. Refugees carried it into Greece.

    Wikipedia: Zeybek (dance)

  • The Metaxas dictatorship of the 1930s censored Greek music, including rebetiko lyrics and recordings.

    The Metaxas Project

  • The music of Mikis Theodorakis was banned inside Greece by the 1967 to 1974 military dictatorship.

    Greek Reporter

  • The Orpheus Hellenic Folklore Society in the Chicago area teaches dances from across Greece to adults and younger generations.

    Orpheus Hellenic Folklore Society

Interpretation

  • The story of Anthony Quinn's injured foot shaping the dance comes from Quinn's own later account. It is widely repeated, but it is his recollection.

    Wikipedia: Sirtaki

Several sources are general references. KRIOS lists them as the film relied on them.
