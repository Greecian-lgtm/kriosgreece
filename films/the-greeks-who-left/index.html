The Greece they left

Have you ever heard someone in your family mention a village in Greece, or an island, and realized you never asked what life there was really like? Maybe you were young. Maybe you thought there would always be time. And then one day, there wasn't. You remember the name of the place. Maybe you even know where it is on a map. But what was an ordinary day like there?

Who lived next door? Where did everyone gather? What did home mean to them? We don't always think to ask those questions when the people who know the answers are sitting right beside us. And for so many Greek families, there's another question underneath all of them. Why did they leave? There isn't one answer. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, many Greeks left because work was scarce and they hoped to build a more secure future.

Some crossed the Atlantic to the United States, planning to earn money and return. Later, the hardship and upheaval surrounding the Second World War and the Greek Civil War shaped another generation's decisions. Greeks also made new lives in Australia, Canada, Germany, and elsewhere. Different years. Different destinations. Different reasons. And not everyone who left had the same experience. Some traveled alone. Some followed relatives who had already gone. Some returned to Greece.

Others expected to. They never did. Imagine making that decision without knowing how long you'd be away. What would you take? Clothes. Papers. An address written down carefully. Maybe a photograph. But the hardest things to leave weren't always things you could pack. The voices you heard every day. The familiar walk through your neighborhood. The people who knew your family before you were born. In Greek, there's a word for life far from home: ξενιτιά.

It's more than a destination on a map. It's the distance between the person who leaves and the people who stay. In Epirus, traditional songs spoke of that separation. One is called Ξενιτεμένο μου πουλί. It means, roughly, my bird, far from home. And that part of the story matters, too. For every person beginning again somewhere else, there could be people back home waiting for news, a letter, or the next visit.

The life they built

Today, we might find a trace of that journey in an old passenger list. A name. An age. A place of origin. A destination. At first, it's just a document. Until you recognize the name. Maybe the spelling is different. Maybe there's a village nobody ever mentioned. Maybe an address points to a relative who arrived earlier. One small detail can bring you closer to someone you thought you knew only through family stories.

But a passenger list tells you where a person was going. It doesn't tell you what happened when they got there. Think about arriving in a place where the language is unfamiliar. You need work. Somewhere to live. You have to learn how things are done. Even the little things nobody explains. And you still have people back home wondering how you're getting along. For many newcomers, other Greeks helped make that first step less lonely.

Someone might know about a job. Someone might offer a place to stay or introduce you to a neighborhood, a church, or a familiar shop. Over time, communities took shape. In Chicago, and in cities across the world, Greeks opened businesses, formed associations, built churches, and found ways to gather. Those places weren't just reminders of home. They were part of making a new home. And something interesting happened along the way.

The Greece people remembered didn't stay frozen exactly as they'd left it. It changed with their new lives. A family might speak Greek at home and English at work. Children might grow up with customs their classmates didn't know. Holidays and celebrations might look familiar, but take place thousands of miles from the village where their parents grew up. It wasn't always easy to balance those worlds. But you could hear that connection in a greeting.

See it in an icon on the wall. Taste it in something made for a family gathering. And before long, those ordinary details became part of someone else's childhood.

The Greece that stayed

Maybe that's the part of this story you recognize. The next generation didn't necessarily remember the journey. They remembered the people who made a life after it. A grandparent calling them by a nickname nobody else used. Tavli dice hitting the table. A komboloi clicking in someone's hand. The smell of coffee in the kitchen. A familiar expression in Greek that everybody understood, even if nobody could explain it in English.

Maybe there was an icon in the corner of the room. A name-day call. A meal that appeared every time the family came together. Or a song that made everyone stop talking for a moment, and then start singing. If you've watched My Big Fat Greek Wedding, you know the exaggerated version of Greek family life: the relatives, the food, everyone having an opinion. It's a comedy. But for some people, there was something recognizable underneath the jokes.

Of course, no two families were the same. Some children learned Greek. Others understood more than they could speak. Some families were deeply involved in church and community life. Others kept only a few customs, or made entirely new ones. And that doesn't make one family's story more Greek than another's. What stayed was often what fit into everyday life. A recipe learned by watching, not measuring. A story told at the table.

A place name mentioned so often you felt you knew it, even if you'd never been there. When we're young, these things can seem so ordinary that we barely notice them. Then years pass. We get older. We begin asking different questions. Who were our parents and grandparents before they became the people we knew? What did they give up? What did they hope for? What was their home like before ours?

Maybe you know the island, but not the village. Maybe there's a face in an old photograph and nobody remembers the name. Maybe you can make the dish, but you don't know who taught it to the person who taught you. And suddenly, something familiar becomes a mystery. That feeling isn't limited to Greek families. It's one of the ways we discover that our own lives are part of a much longer story.

What we carry forward

The good news is, we don't need every answer to begin. We can start with one question. Ask someone where the family came from. Not just Greece. Which town, which village, which neighborhood? Ask who left first. Who stayed behind. Whether anyone went back. Look through photographs together. Write down the names while someone still remembers them. And ask about the little things. The words people used. The games they played.

The meals they made without recipes. Sometimes a memory leads to a document. Sometimes a document leads to another question. And sometimes we won't find the answer. We shouldn't invent one. The people who left Greece didn't carry an entire country with them in a suitcase. They carried pieces of a life, and built something new from them. Their children carried different pieces. And so did the generations after that.

Some traditions survived. Some changed. Others faded. But Greek culture didn't end when people crossed a border. It continued in the homes they made, the communities they built, and the things they passed on, sometimes without even realizing it. We don't have to recreate the past to feel connected to it. We can listen. We can ask. We can learn where those familiar pieces came from. Maybe for you it begins with a photograph.

Or a village name you've heard all your life. Or maybe it's something simpler. A meal at the family table. You know the taste. You remember who made it. But have you ever asked where that recipe began? That's where we'll go next.

Ask someone in your family

Questions to start a conversation at your own table.

  • What did our family bring with them when they left Greece?
  • What did our grandparents miss most about home?
  • Which traditions did our family keep after arriving in a new country?

What the sources tell us

Established

  • The first wave of emigration followed the economic crisis of 1893, when currant export prices collapsed. From 1890 to 1914 almost a sixth of Greece's population emigrated, mostly to the United States and Egypt.

    Migration Policy Institute, Greece: A History of Migration (Kasimis and Kassimi, 2004)

  • More than one million Greeks emigrated in a second wave, mainly between 1950 and 1974, most to Western Europe, the United States, Canada and Australia. Between 1974 and 1985 almost half of them returned to Greece.

    Migration Policy Institute, Greece: A History of Migration (Kasimis and Kassimi, 2004)

Still open

  • How many Greeks reached the United States in the first wave. Published histories give figures from about 430,000 to more than 600,000, so the film says "hundreds of thousands".

    KRIOS research notes
