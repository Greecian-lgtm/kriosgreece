Did Leonidas Know About the Anopaea Path Before Thermopylae?

What the Primary Sources Tell Us

Herodotus claims the Greeks knew about the Anopaea mountain path from the beginning of the siege. However, he also records that Ephialtes revealed its exact location to Xerxes on the second day of fighting. This contradiction matters more than scholars usually admit. If Leonidas truly knew the route beforehand, why would a Persian traitor's information on day two change everything?

The Timing Problem

The evidence suggests Leonidas was attempting a genuine defensive stand, not a predetermined death march. He likely knew a flanking route existed somewhere in those mountains—Greek commanders weren't ignorant of their own territory. What he didn't know was that Ephialtes would hand the Persians the precise passage. That betrayal on day two forced Leonidas's actual final decision. The glorious last stand wasn't his original plan.

What Got Lost in Translation

Herodotus filtered everything through what he heard decades later. The narrative we inherited treats knowledge of the path as definite, but primary sources show uncertainty. Leonidas responded to Ephialtes's betrayal as shocking news, not confirmation of what he already knew. The battle's outcome changed because of that second-day revelation, not because of advance knowledge.

The Propaganda Layer

Within months of the battle, Simonides was writing the famous epitaph: "Go tell the Spartans, stranger passing by." The mythmaking happened immediately, not centuries later. By the time the legend solidified, the messy reality—that Leonidas made tactical decisions in real time rather than orchestrating predetermined heroism—had already been smoothed away.

The honest answer: probably not. Leonidas likely knew flanking routes existed generally. He didn't know Ephialtes would betray the specific path. That's what made day two a catastrophe instead of a manageable retreat.
