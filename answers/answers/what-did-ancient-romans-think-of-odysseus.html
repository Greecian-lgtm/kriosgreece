What Did Ancient Romans Think of Odysseus?

Primary Sources

Cicero offers the most direct Roman assessment. In De Officiis, written around 44 BCE, Cicero explicitly calls Odysseus a liar and a coward. This wasn't casual dismissal—Cicero was establishing philosophical arguments about virtue and character, and Odysseus served as a cautionary example of cunning without moral foundation.

Virgil's Aeneid provides another crucial perspective. Writing in the late first century BCE, Virgil portrays Odysseus as essentially a war criminal. In Book 2, the Trojan Aeneas recounts how Odysseus massacred innocents during the fall of Troy. Virgil deliberately contrasts Aeneas—the dutiful hero—with Odysseus, whose resourcefulness came at the expense of ethics and human life.

What This Reveals

The Romans didn't see Odysseus as a misunderstood hero waiting for modern reinterpretation. They recognized immediately what Homer showed: a man whose intelligence was divorced from virtue. To Cicero and Virgil, Odysseus represented a particular danger—the clever operator who prioritizes survival over honor, pragmatism over principle.

This matters because it corrects a modern misconception. We sometimes treat critical readings of Odysseus as recent discoveries, products of twentieth-century scholarship uncovering hidden depths. The truth is simpler and stranger: the ancients were already having this debate two thousand years ago. They didn't need our translations or our theory. They read Homer and saw what he actually wrote.

Roman writers inherited Greek literature but weren't bound by Greek reverence. They could afford to be blunt. Odysseus was useful as a philosophical counterexample precisely because his failures were so visible to anyone reading carefully. The Aeneid exists partly as a corrective—showing what heroism looks like when it's coupled with duty rather than cunning alone.

The Consensus

Ancient Rome had no shortage of Odysseus apologists. But the most serious thinkers—the ones writing philosophy and epic poetry—saw him clearly. He was clever. He survived. He was not, by their lights, truly admirable.
