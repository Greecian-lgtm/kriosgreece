What is the meaning of ate in Greek?

Ate is ruin—the blind folly that the gods send to destroy you. It's not sin or fate or bad luck. It's active divine punishment wrapped up in the form of a compulsion that clouds your judgment so thoroughly you can't see what you're doing until it's too late.

The primary source

Homer gives us the oldest recorded definition in the Iliad (19.91-94). Agamemnon is trying to explain why he took Achilles's prize, his captive Briseis. He doesn't say "I chose poorly." He says ate seized him—a goddess herself, older than Zeus, who walks on soft feet and destroys men's minds. He was blind. Not metaphorically. Blind.

Here's what Homer actually has him say: ate is a daughter of Zeus, "delicate of foot, and never touching the ground. She walks upon the heads of men, bringing ruin, and she entangles one or the other of us."

This is crucial. Ate isn't your mistake. She's the cause of your mistake. The gods use her to punish hubris—and the punishment works by making you stupid at exactly the moment you need to think clearly.

What most people get wrong

People confuse ate with atē (the modern Greek word for "shame"). They also confuse it with hamartia—the "tragic flaw" from later Greek drama. Neither is right. Ate isn't internal. She comes from outside. The gods send her. And she doesn't reveal a flaw you already had—she creates the temporary blindness that makes you commit one.

This confusion comes from centuries of Greek tragedy writers (Sophocles, Euripides) who reinterpreted the old myths. By their time, ate had become more psychological. But Homer's ate is a force. A weapon. A sent thing.

Why it matters

When Agamemnon blames ate for taking Briseis, he's not making an excuse. He's naming the moment when the gods decided to punish his arrogance by making him arrogant. The Trojan War, ten years of death—it flows from this moment of ate-induced blindness. One bad decision made while your mind isn't yours.

Primary sources

  • Homer, Iliad 19.91-94 — Agamemnon's speech, the oldest source for ate as divine ruin
  • Hesiod, Works and Days 230-31 — ate as the daughter of Zeus and Themis
  • Later reinterpretation: Sophocles, Ajax — ate begins to shift toward internal tragic flaw
